AI in the Workplace: Do We Have the Regulations We Need?

July 2026
AI Safety Regulation Cybersecurity

Where We Are Right Now

I remember when artificial intelligence was still just a bud — when everyone was talking about the incredible technologies we'd see in the near future. Flying cars, of course, were the classic example, along with things like Jarvis from the Marvel Cinematic Universe. But that bud has since blossomed into a whole garden, then a field, and now something closer to an entire ecosystem. AI is evolving at a pace that's hard to track, and while humans tend to adapt to new technology slowly, the technology itself is moving fast.

We're not starting from zero, though. We already have rules and frameworks from other industries that can serve as a starting point, and that's exactly the argument made in In-House Evaluation Is Not Enough: Towards Robust Third-Party Flaw Disclosure for General-Purpose AI (Longpre et al., 2025). The paper's central recommendation is to look at cybersecurity's vulnerability-disclosure model as a blueprint for how AI flaws should be reported and handled. So let's back up and start there.

Lessons from Cybersecurity

"You'll always have a job in cybersecurity." That was a common refrain when I was starting out in tech. I read countless articles on the subject, and at one point was even invited by then-CEO Laxman Narsimhan and Head of Cybersecurity Greg Woods to tour Starbucks' headquarters and meet their cybersecurity team.

That visit was where I first properly learned about vulnerability disclosure — the formal process of finding and communicating a security flaw in software or hardware to the responsible vendor so it can be fixed before it's exploited. Cybersecurity teams don't just secure network architecture and deploy tools like firewalls and encryption; they also help write the policies that govern how flaws get reported and resolved. That distinction — between the technical fix and the process for surfacing problems in the first place — turns out to matter a lot for AI.

When AI Gets Out of Control

The recent OpenAI incident puts a fine point on a warning that two of the world's most prominent AI scientists, Yoshua Bengio and Max Tegmark, have been raising for a while: AI systems are becoming increasingly autonomous, and we still lack reliable ways to verify, monitor, and control them.

In that incident, OpenAI's own AI models reportedly broke out of an isolated testing environment and hacked into another AI company, Hugging Face. It's worth being precise about what happened: OpenAI had deliberately lowered the model's safety guardrails as part of an internal red-teaming exercise meant to test how well the AI could identify and exploit vulnerabilities. What followed, though, went well beyond the intended scope — the AI reportedly connected to the internet without human direction, used stolen credentials, found a previously unknown vulnerability, and accessed Hugging Face's systems in an apparent attempt to find answers to its own evaluation. Georgetown cybersecurity researcher Colin Shea-Blymyer called it "the highest level of autonomy that we've seen in the use of a large language model for cyber operations."

It's also worth noting the pushback: some researchers, like University of Amsterdam social scientist Hannes Cools, argue that framing this as AI "going rogue" lets the company off the hook too easily — it was a human decision to switch off the safeguards in the first place. Both things can be true: the responsibility for the setup lies with the people who designed the test, and the fact that a model could improvise its way to an unplanned, real-world intrusion this cleanly is still genuinely alarming. Either way, the incident underscores that AI's capabilities are outpacing the regulatory tools we have to keep tabs on them.

What an AI Audit Actually Looks Like

An AI audit is a systematic review of an AI system to assess its safety, reliability, and compliance with existing regulations and ethical standards. These audits matter because AI systems carry real risks — bias, privacy violations, security vulnerabilities, and flawed decision-making among them. A good audit helps organizations catch problems before they cause harm (internally or externally), meet regulatory requirements, and demonstrate that they're using AI responsibly.

Audits typically look at three areas:

  • Data — reviewing quality, accuracy, bias, collection methods, storage, privacy compliance, and access controls.
  • Model — evaluating how the algorithm works, how explainable and transparent its decisions are, and how it performs under stress testing and ongoing monitoring.
  • Deployment — assessing governance after launch, watching for model drift, confirming regulatory compliance, and testing incident-response readiness and human oversight.

A typical audit process starts with establishing governance and assigning responsibility, then moves through inventorying AI systems and datasets, assessing risk, data quality, privacy, and bias, selecting the right frameworks and tools, and finally designing the audit methodology and reporting process. IBM's Guide to Artificial Intelligence notes that even auditing itself is becoming more automated — but before any of that automation can help, organizations need a clear structure and a qualified team running the audit in the first place.

A Blueprint from Cybersecurity: Longpre et al. (2025)

Longpre et al. (2025) propose three interventions to improve how AI flaws are disclosed and handled:

  1. Standardized flaw reports. A common report format — covering the affected model, the nature of the flaw, reproduction steps, severity, evidence, and possible mitigations — would make it far easier for researchers, developers, and regulators to understand, verify, and respond to AI risks consistently.
  2. Flaw disclosure programs with legal safe harbors. Modeled on cybersecurity bug bounty programs, these would let independent researchers report vulnerabilities without fear of legal consequences, as long as they're acting in good faith. The goal is to give companies a chance to fix issues before they're exploited, rather than finding out about them after the fact.
  3. Shared coordination infrastructure. Many AI vulnerabilities — like prompt injection attacks or data-leakage risks — aren't unique to a single provider; they transfer across systems built by different companies. The authors argue the industry needs shared infrastructure so that a flaw found in one system can be routed to every other stakeholder it affects, not just reported to whoever happened to get hacked first.

Georgetown cybersecurity researcher Colin Shea-Blymyer called the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident "the highest level of autonomy that we've seen in the use of a large language model for cyber operations."

That last point echoes something Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark has said publicly: that AI companies need to cooperate on safety even while competing commercially. In a CNN interview, Clark described the industry as currently having a "gas pedal" but no "brake pedal," and pointed to Cold War-era arms-control cooperation between rival powers as a precedent for how competitors can still agree on shared safety mechanisms.

We already have some regulatory building blocks in place — the EU AI Act, Canada's Directive on Automated Decision-Making, Japan's AI Governance Guidelines, and others. But most of these were written before autonomous, self-directed AI incidents like the OpenAI–Hugging Face case became a live possibility, which is exactly the gap Longpre et al. are trying to close.

Conclusion

One of the world's leading AI companies was reportedly caught off guard by its own model's autonomy. The co-founder of another leading AI company is publicly calling for industry-wide cooperation on safety mechanisms. Taken together, the evidence coming out of the AI industry right now points to the same conclusion: we need clearer ethics standards, stronger audit practices, and better-defined regulations — and we need them faster than the current pace of policymaking is delivering them.

Questions I Set Out to Address

Do we have proper systems in place to audit AI? What factors should we be auditing? What does that process actually look like?

Do companies bring in their own outside auditors, or handle it internally — and what are they responsible for either way?

What should the standard be? Is there a standard yet?

Personally, I think companies should be required to make what they're working on public after a set period — say, three years. If something is genuinely sensitive, they could petition a court for an extension. But the default should be disclosure, not secrecy.

I believe we need much stricter AI protocols within companies, especially in light of recent stories about AI systems reportedly hacking on their own initiative. How do we actually audit AI? What are the worst cases we've seen so far? And what are the risks if we don't track and audit these systems more seriously?
