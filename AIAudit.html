AI in the Workplace: Do We Have the Regulations We Need?

Written April 2026 · Edited July 2026
AI Safety Regulation Cybersecurity

Where We Are Right Now

I recall at the rise of artificial intelligence or just when it was a bud and everyone was talking about some incredibly advanced technologies we’re going to see in the (near) future. Flying cars of course was a popular concept, technologies we envisioned like Jarvis from the Marvel Cinematic Universe and more. But that bud has since blossomed into a whole garden, then a field, and now something closer to an entire ecosystem. AI is evolving at a pace that’s hard to track, and while humans tend to adapt to a new technology slowly, the technology itself is moving fast.

We’re not starting from zero, though. We already have rules and framework set up from adjacent industries which can serve as starting points. This is the exact argument made in In House Evaluation Is Not Enough: Towards Robust Third-Party Flaw Disclosure for General-Purpose AI. It largely recommends referring back to the cybersecurity vulnerability disclosure as a blueprint. Let’s backtrack to cybersecurity.

Lessons from Cybersecurity

“You’ll always have a job in cybersecurity.” It was such a popular sentence as I was beginning my career in technology. I read countless articles on the subject and was even invited by the CEO at the time Laxman Narsimhan and Head of Cybersecurity of Starbucks, Greg Woods to tour the headquarters, meet the Cybersecurity team and learn about the work they do.

The visit was where I first properly learned about vulnerability disclosure, the formal process of finding and communicating a security flaw in software or hardware to the responsible vendor so it can be mended prior to exploitation. Cybersecurity teams don’t just secure network architecture and deploy tools like firewalls and encryption; they also help write the policies that govern how flaws get reported and resolved. That distinction between technical fix and process for surfacing problems in the first place, turns out to matter a lot for AI.

When AI Gets Out of Control

The recent OpenAI incident displays a clear issue brought up by two of the world's most prominent AI scientists, Yoshua Bengio and Max Tegmark: Artificial intelligence is becoming increasingly autonomous, and we severely lack metrics to regulate, verify and control these systems. OpenAI’s hack was essentially AI escaping a testing environment and hacking another company’s system. It was a full human decision to take down the guard rails and while OpenAI states this was done on purpose to test how well the AI models could identify and exploit vulnerabilities. However, the events which followed were quite advanced, unplanned for and a bit scary. The AI allegedly escaped its intended testing environment, connected to the internet without any human direction, used stolen credentials, discovered previously unknown software, accessed (Hugging Face’s) systems and attempted to obtain confidential information.

Georgetown cybersecurity researcher Colin Shea-Blymer described it as “the highest level of autonomy seen in AI-assisted cyber operations.” However, some researchers like University of Amsterdam social scientist Hannes Cools argues real responsibility lies with the system designers not the AI. And while they disagreed that this is a form of AI going rogue due to the deliberately reduced safety guardrails and overall instructions the point of AI’s development and progression moving at speeds and variances regulations aren’t matching still stands.

What an AI Audit Actually Looks Like

An AI audit consists of a systematic review of an AI system to determine its safety, reliability and whether it is ethical and compliant with existing regulations. They’re incredibly important as artificial intelligence heavily includes risks, bias, privacy violations, security vulnerabilities and incorrect decisions. Audits aid organizations in detecting problems prior to harm (internal and external), meeting regulatory requirements, demonstrating responsible AI use.

Data audits focus on data, models and deployment. In data, audits review the quality, accuracy, bias, collection, storage, privacy compliance, access controls and more. When reviewing the model’s aspect, audits often evaluate how algorithms work, explainability and transparency, performance monitoring, stress testing and more. Finally, deployment assesses governance after launch, continuous monitoring for model drift, regulatory compliance, incident response readiness and human oversight. The current existing AI audit process typically includes establishing governance and assigning responsibilities, inventory AI systems and datasets, assessing risks, data quality, privacy and bias, selecting appropriate audit frameworks and tools, designing audit methodology and reporting process. However, IBM’s Guide to Artificial Intelligence states how even AI auditing is to become more automated. Prior to taking such steps it’s important to establish a strong AI auditor.

Longpre et al. (2025) propose three interventions to improve artificial intelligence flaw disclosure. First recommendation consists of stressing the importance of a new standardized AI flaw report. These would go into deep detail regarding the vulnerabilities of the affected model, nature of the flaw, reproduction steps, severity, evidence and possible mitigations. They argue that standardization would make it a bit simpler for researchers and developers to comprehend, verify and respond to AI risks. Second, a flaw disclosure program (or several) with safe legal spaces for independent researchers to report vulnerabilities without fear of legal consequences when acting responsibly. This is quite similar to cybersecurity bug bounty programs, and these initiatives would encourage researchers to disclose issues and ensure organizations can address them prior to exploitation. Finally, Longpre et al. call for infrastructure to support coordinated disclosure enabling AI companies, researchers and other stakeholders to collaborate when addressing flaws especially as several vulnerabilities such as prompt injection attacks data leakage risks and more affect multiple AI systems rather than a single provider. This last point echoes Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark's perspective, stressing how crucial it is for the entire industry of artificial intelligence to collaborate despite being competitors. He insists “we must work together and implement industry wide brake pedals”

Conclusion

We have a few existing ones such as EU AI Act, Canada’s Directiveness on Automated Decision-Making, Japan’s AI Governance Guidelines and more. However, most were established prior to self-directed AI incidents like the OpenAI-Hugging Face case became a live possibility which is precisely the gap Longpre et al. are trying to bridge.

One complication the cybersecurity analogy does not account for accurately is how the disclosure model assumes there's a single vendor responsible for the fix. This may hold for closed models such as Claude or ChatGPT where companies keep the underlying model private and accessible solely through an API or chat interface. However, for open-weight models in which companies release the model file this means whatever issue/ bug exists would be scattered across the internet in infinite copies with no party which can debug all. I wonder if an "update" from the original company would work as a solution, I don't know if there's a clean answer yet but I will research and or write it into existence.

A top AI company was astonished by its own AI’s autonomy; the co-founder of another top AI company states we must all work together to implement regulations. All the information coming out about artificial intelligence points to the need of AI ethics, restrictions and regulations and they're needed faster than the current pace of policymaking delivering them.

Questions I Set Out to Address

Do we have proper systems in place to audit AI? What factors should we be auditing? What does that process actually look like?

Do companies bring in their own outside auditors, or handle it internally — and what are they responsible for either way?

What should the standard be? Is there a standard yet?

Personally, I think companies should be required to make what they're working on public after a set period — say, three years. If something is genuinely sensitive, they could petition a court for an extension. But the default should be disclosure, not secrecy.

I believe we need much stricter AI protocols within companies, especially in light of recent stories about AI systems reportedly hacking on their own initiative. How do we actually audit AI? What are the worst cases we've seen so far? And what are the risks if we don't track and audit these systems more seriously?
