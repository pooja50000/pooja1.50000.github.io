Socioeconomic Effects of AI

July 2026
Economics Labor Policy

"AI is taking our jobs." We've all heard some version of this statement, whether it's "AI is taking over," "AI is taking our jobs," or something else along those lines. While we surf, swim, or nearly drown in this wave of a recession, it feels like the only thing we hear when we come up for air. What if I told you this is simply not true?

According to Dan Katz, First Deputy Managing Director of the IMF, in Artificial Intelligence and the Economics of Adjustment, AI is unlikely to permanently reduce employment. He predicts it will instead shift the task composition, skill requirements, and organizational structure of workplaces. While AI may change many jobs, it should also increase productivity, which can drive economic growth, wages, and living standards. Similar to the Congressional Budget Office's framing in Artificial Intelligence and Its Potential Effects on the Economy and the Federal Budget, I believe artificial intelligence is another technological revolution that will cause massive change across society, technology, and the economy alike. My own view aligns with the IMF's: we're currently in the middle of an adjustment period as society is introduced to AI.

Katz makes an argument that deeply resonated with me: whether it was the steam engine, electrification, or computing, each of these technologies displaced jobs, but each also stimulated new demand — what economists call the Jevons paradox. Katz refers to the fear that AI must be different this time as a modern version of the "lump-of-labor fallacy": the mistaken belief that there's a fixed amount of work to go around, so that whatever AI does, humans simply do less of. History disagrees. New technologies tend to create jobs, just often in sectors that didn't exist yet.

A key part of this transition must involve the aid of companies. Rather than laying off large parts of their workforce, I believe companies should hold training sessions, courses, and information sessions around AI usage. We need to build broader knowledge around this technology to make the transition smoother. While AI may eliminate certain tasks, the goal should not be protecting outdated positions, but helping employees and businesses adapt to new opportunities. As the IMF states, "The goal of policy should not be to protect specific jobs, companies, or industries. It should be to incentivize employees and businesses to adapt."

Through analyzing various articles and incorporating perspectives from both employers and employees, I've identified three key steps to guide a successful AI transition. The first is building cross-functional teams to explore where AI can help. The second is focusing on building trust with employees. The third is creating dedicated AI-literacy training to improve productivity and workplace relationships.

MIT economist and 2024 Nobel laureate Daron Acemoglu takes a more cautious view. He argues that while AI has real long-term potential, its near-term economic benefits are likely to be more gradual and modest than most forecasts suggest, unless AI gets meaningfully better at supporting complex, real-world work. In his view, technology should be redirected toward providing accurate, reliable, task-specific information that helps solve real-world problems, rather than chasing more general capabilities.

According to Acemoglu's estimations, artificial intelligence will only increase U.S. GDP by approximately 1.1 percent over the next ten years, with productivity gains of roughly 0.7 percent — a fraction of the projections from Goldman Sachs and McKinsey.

These statistics genuinely surprised me. While I understand not all tasks can be handed to AI, I suspected it would affect them to a greater extent. Acemoglu argues that although AI may influence nearly 20 percent of tasks in the U.S. labor market, only about 5 percent are likely to be profitably automated soon. The CBO's own report echoes this same note of caution about how little we actually know. It cites survey data showing that only about 5 percent of U.S. businesses currently rely on AI in any meaningful way to produce goods or services, and that while 80 percent of the U.S. workforce could have at least one-tenth of their tasks affected by AI, only 19 percent would see at least half their tasks affected. In other words, broad exposure to AI is common, but deep disruption to any single job is far rarer. This highlights that AI's economic impact depends not only on what the technology can do, but on whether adopting it is practical and economically worthwhile.

What Jobs Can AI Learn? Measuring Exposure by Reinforcement Learning takes this a step further, introducing a Reinforcement Learning Feasibility Index designed to estimate which occupations are most likely to be automated as AI's capabilities improve — rather than estimating how exposed occupations are to today's large language models alone. Authors Phillip Moreira Tomei and Bouke Klein Teeselink argue that current measures are limited because they focus solely on present-day capabilities and don't account for future improvements. Their framework scored 17,951 individual O*NET tasks across 894 occupations, first classifying whether each required physical or digital work, then scoring the digital tasks and aggregating the results to the occupation level.

Prior to scoring, each task had to pass a physical feasibility gate: tasks requiring real physical embodiment failed automatically and received a score of zero, which occurred for 41 percent of all tasks. Tasks that passed were scored across eight dimensions covering how verifiable the outcome is, how simulable the environment is, and how much the task resembles a clean, gradable decision problem, then aggregated to the occupation level. The results diverge sharply from other AI exposure measures. Jobs like musicians, CEOs, and microbiologists seem highly exposed to AI overall, but score low on RL feasibility, since verifying their work is difficult. Jobs like gas plant operators and railroad conductors show the opposite: less exposed by typical measures, but highly RL-feasible, since their work involves clear, checkable decisions.

A point I hadn't fully considered before is how much the cost of implementing AI may outweigh its benefits. Acemoglu notes that today's AI performs best on simple, predictable tasks — pure pattern recognition with clear success metrics. Many complex jobs, though, such as teaching or electrical work, vary from situation to situation and require context, judgment, and problem-solving skills that current AI still lacks. I'd always assumed AI would eventually "take over" in some far-off, futuristic sense, and that we're simply in the transition period now. What I hadn't given enough thought to was the cost of that transition. Because the technology is still so new, implementation costs may be extremely high right now, but I believe those costs will come down as adoption becomes more common on both the development and deployment sides.

This same cost analysis shows up in PwC's Responsible AI: Now that AI is part of your business, what does it mean for audits? Even when a task is technically automatable, PwC notes that adopting AI brings real overhead costs that companies must absorb before seeing any return. This includes documentation, monitoring, and internal review for every new use case.

Artificial intelligence isn't destroying jobs or creating them wholesale. It's shifting the size and shape of current employment, and its economic impact depends less on the technology's raw capability than on whether transition costs are managed adequately, whether companies invest in training over layoffs, and whether policies focus on helping people adjust. If done right, this new wave of technology may be difficult in the short run, but genuinely positive in the long run.
